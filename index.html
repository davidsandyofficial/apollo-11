    May 25, 1961

    A Promise to Congress

    President John F. Kennedy asks the United States to commit to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to Earth before the end of the decade. The country has a total of about 15 minutes of human spaceflight experience.

    July 16, 1969 · 9:32 a.m. EDT

    Liftoff

    The Saturn V, about 363 feet tall, the tallest and most powerful rocket flown at the time, rises from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center. Aboard: Neil Armstrong, Michael Collins and Edwin "Buzz" Aldrin.

    July 16 to 19, 1969

    Three Days Across the Dark

    After one and a half orbits of Earth, the third stage fires again to send the crew toward the Moon. The command module Columbia and lunar module Eagle coast for about three days before braking into lunar orbit on July 19.

    July 20, 1969

    Powered Descent

    Armstrong and Aldrin undock in Eagle while Collins stays in orbit aboard Columbia. On the way down, the guidance computer flashes 1202 and 1201 program alarms. Seeing a boulder-strewn crater ahead, Armstrong takes semi-manual control and flies on to find a safe spot as fuel runs low.

    July 21, 1969

    Two and a Half Hours on the Moon

    Aldrin joins Armstrong about 19 minutes later. Together they plant the U.S. flag, deploy experiments including a laser reflector still used today, and collect about 47.5 pounds of lunar rock and soil. A plaque on Eagle's leg reads: "We came in peace for all mankind."

    July 24, 1969

    Splashdown

    After rejoining Collins in lunar orbit, the crew heads home. Columbia splashes down in the Pacific Ocean and is recovered by USS Hornet. Mission time: 8 days, 3 hours, 18 minutes.
